Effects of surface mass flux on the stability and receptivity of the rotor
boundary layer in a rotor–stator system
Haojie Zhang 1, 2 and Jianxin Liu 1, 2
1)Department of Mechanics, Tianjin University, Tianjin 300072, China
2)National Key Laboratory of V ehicle Power System, Tianjin 300072, China
(*Corresponding author: shookware@tju.edu.cn)
(Dated: 13 September 2026)
ABSTRACT
Rotor–stator configurations are common in turbomachinery, where rotating boundary layers may undergo instability
and transition. A local linear framework is developed to determine how surface mass flux modifies the stability and
receptivity of the rotor boundary layer in a rotor–stator cavity. The rotating core provides an outer flow condition that
differs from that over an isolated rotating disk. The base flow, convective and absolute instabilities, and receptivity are
analyzed under injection, zero surface mass flux, and suction. Injection thickens the rotor boundary layer, expands the
unstable domain of the inviscid Type I mode, and increases its growth rate. At representative conditions, the growth rate
of the viscous Type II mode is less sensitive to surface mass flux than that of the Type I mode. Contributions from the
base flow and its gradients largely cancel for the Type II mode. The representative Type I adjoint mode is concentrated
near the critical layer. The principal Type II peak occurs where the effective shear vanishes. A radially localized
wall displacement with a specified azimuthal mode number represents stationary roughness or unsteady wall forcing.
Combining local receptivity with spatial growth gives the downstream disturbance level. The radius of maximum local
receptivity does not necessarily produce the largest downstream amplitude because the available growth interval also
matters.
Keywords: Linear stability, rotor boundary layer, surface mass flux, rotor–stator system, adjoint receptivity
I. INTRODUCTION
Rotating disk boundary layers occur in turbomachinery
and disk cavities, where rotation controls friction, heat trans-
fer, and fluid transport. They also form a canonical three-
dimensional boundary layer whose stability is influenced by
the combined effects of crossflow, rotation, and viscosity.
V on Kármán obtained the similarity solution for a disk rotat-
ing beneath otherwise stationary fluid1. Gregory et al. subse-
quently identified the crossflow instability and related it to the
spiral vortices observed during transition2. Later experiments
and reviews established the rotating disk as a model problem
for studying instability and transition in three-dimensional
boundary layers3,4.
The local spectrum of the rotating disk contains several
spatial branches, of which Type I and Type II are the prin-
cipal unstable modes considered here. Type I is an inviscid
crossflow mode associated with an inflectional velocity pro-
file. Type II has a viscous origin and is influenced by the Cori-
olis force and streamline curvature2,5,6. Malik determined the
neutral curve of stationary disturbances, while Hall derived
the asymptotic structures of the stationary Type I and Type II
modes5,7. Balakumar and Malik extended the analysis to trav-
eling disturbances, and Balakumar et al. examined their re-
ceptivity and nonparallel development8,9. These studies show
that frequency and azimuthal mode number change both the
unstable range and the spatial structure of the two modes.
A rotor–stator cavity introduces a second wall and changes
the fluid outside the rotor layer. In the Batchelor regime, an
Ekman-type layer forms on the rotor, a Bödewadt-type layer
forms on the stator, and a rotating core lies between them10–12.
The finite core velocity and its radial pressure gradient distin-
guish the rotor layer from the boundary layer on an isolated
disk. Combined numerical and theoretical calculations by
Serre et al. identified separate rotor and stator instability fam-
ilies, and subsequent experiments and simulations examined
transition in the confined flow 13–15. These studies show that
Type II becomes unstable closer to the axis, whereas Type I
can attain a larger growth rate over part of the rotor layer.
Global analyses and large eddy simulations have also iden-
tified sustained cavity modes and examined their control16–18.
The core flow therefore changes not only the base velocity
profiles but also the radial interval over which each instability
can develop.
Surface mass flux provides another means of modifying ro-
tating boundary layers. Injection and suction alter the layer
thickness, wall shear, velocity gradients, and transport normal
to the wall. Global calculations for an isolated rotating disk
and local analyses of Bödewadt–Ekman–von Kármán flows
have shown that mass transfer can shift instability bound-
aries and change the global response19,20. Because Type I and
Type II arise from different mechanisms, changes in the base
velocity and its gradients need not affect them equally. Addi-
tionally, in a rotor–stator cavity, surface mass flux influences
core rotation and the radial pressure balance. Addressing the
combined influence of confinement and mass flux on the two
rotor modes therefore requires a rotor–stator formulation.
