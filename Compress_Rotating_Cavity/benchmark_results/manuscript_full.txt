Effects of surface mass flux on the stability and receptivity of the rotor
boundary layer in a rotor–stator system
Haojie Zhang 1, 2 and Jianxin Liu 1, 2
1)Department of Mechanics, Tianjin University, Tianjin 300072, China
2)National Key Laboratory of V ehicle Power System, Tianjin 300072, China

ABSTRACT
Rotor–stator configurations are common in turbomachinery, where rotating boundary layers may undergo instability
and transition. A local linear framework is developed to determine how surface mass flux modifies the stability and
receptivity of the rotor boundary layer in a rotor–stator cavity. The rotating core provides an outer flow condition that
differs from that over an isolated rotating disk. The base flow, convective and absolute instabilities, and receptivity are
analyzed under injection, zero surface mass flux, and suction. Injection thickens the rotor boundary layer, expands the
unstable domain of the inviscid Type I mode, and increases its growth rate. At representative conditions, the growth rate
of the viscous Type II mode is less sensitive to surface mass flux than that of the Type I mode. Contributions from the
base flow and its gradients largely cancel for the Type II mode. The representative Type I adjoint mode is concentrated
near the critical layer. The principal Type II peak occurs where the effective shear vanishes. A radially localized
wall displacement with a specified azimuthal mode number represents stationary roughness or unsteady wall forcing.
Combining local receptivity with spatial growth gives the downstream disturbance level. The radius of maximum local
receptivity does not necessarily produce the largest downstream amplitude because the available growth interval also
matters.
Keywords: Linear stability, rotor boundary layer, surface mass flux, rotor–stator system, adjoint receptivity
I. INTRODUCTION
Rotating disk boundary layers occur in turbomachinery
and disk cavities, where rotation controls friction, heat trans-
fer, and fluid transport. They also form a canonical three-
dimensional boundary layer whose stability is influenced by
the combined effects of crossflow, rotation, and viscosity.
V on Kármán obtained the similarity solution for a disk rotat-
ing beneath otherwise stationary fluid1. Gregory et al. subse-
quently identified the crossflow instability and related it to the
spiral vortices observed during transition2. Later experiments
and reviews established the rotating disk as a model problem
for studying instability and transition in three-dimensional
boundary layers3,4.
The local spectrum of the rotating disk contains several
spatial branches, of which Type I and Type II are the prin-
cipal unstable modes considered here. Type I is an inviscid
crossflow mode associated with an inflectional velocity pro-
file. Type II has a viscous origin and is influenced by the Cori-
olis force and streamline curvature2,5,6. Malik determined the
neutral curve of stationary disturbances, while Hall derived
the asymptotic structures of the stationary Type I and Type II
modes5,7. Balakumar and Malik extended the analysis to trav-
eling disturbances, and Balakumar et al. examined their re-
ceptivity and nonparallel development8,9. These studies show
that frequency and azimuthal mode number change both the
unstable range and the spatial structure of the two modes.
A rotor–stator cavity introduces a second wall and changes
the fluid outside the rotor layer. In the Batchelor regime, an
Ekman-type layer forms on the rotor, a Bödewadt-type layer
forms on the stator, and a rotating core lies between them10–12.
The finite core velocity and its radial pressure gradient distin-
guish the rotor layer from the boundary layer on an isolated
disk. Combined numerical and theoretical calculations by
Serre et al. identified separate rotor and stator instability fam-
ilies, and subsequent experiments and simulations examined
transition in the confined flow 13–15. These studies show that
Type II becomes unstable closer to the axis, whereas Type I
can attain a larger growth rate over part of the rotor layer.
Global analyses and large eddy simulations have also iden-
tified sustained cavity modes and examined their control16–18.
The core flow therefore changes not only the base velocity
profiles but also the radial interval over which each instability
can develop.
Surface mass flux provides another means of modifying ro-
tating boundary layers. Injection and suction alter the layer
thickness, wall shear, velocity gradients, and transport normal
to the wall. Global calculations for an isolated rotating disk
and local analyses of Bödewadt–Ekman–von Kármán flows
have shown that mass transfer can shift instability bound-
aries and change the global response19,20. Because Type I and
Type II arise from different mechanisms, changes in the base
velocity and its gradients need not affect them equally. Addi-
tionally, in a rotor–stator cavity, surface mass flux influences
core rotation and the radial pressure balance. Addressing the
combined influence of confinement and mass flux on the two
rotor modes therefore requires a rotor–stator formulation.
The radial development of an unstable disturbance involves
convective, absolute, and global behavior. A convectively
unstable mode grows while it travels away from its source,
whereas a locally absolute instability grows at a fixed radius
in a frozen, parallel flow. Lingwood identified a local abso-
lute threshold for the rotating disk boundary layer through a
spatiotemporal analysis and related it to wave packet behavior\n2
in experiments21,22. Analyses that retain radial variation ad-
dress a complementary question: whether this local behavior
leads to a sustained response in the radially developing flow.
Global impulse response calculations by Davies and Carpen-
ter found a convective response at long times, while Healey
showed that a nonparallel correction can strengthen part of the
local absolute branch23,24. Simulations and impulse response
studies further demonstrated that the global outcome depends
on the radial domain, disturbance structure, and downstream
conditions25–27. Recent work on rotor–stator cavities has like-
wise separated local convective onset, local absolute onset,
and global instability 28. Taken together, these studies show
that local and global analyses characterize different aspects
of instability rather than establishing a single universal con-
sequence of radial nonparallelism. Lingwood’s locally paral-
lel, spatiotemporal pinch point method was subsequently ap-
plied to boundary layers over rotating spheres and cones, to
the BEK family with surface mass flux, and to rotor–stator
configurations13,20,29,30. Its use across these related but dis-
tinct models supports its adoption here as a local diagnostic
for the frozen radius problem; the resulting threshold is used
as a reference radius rather than as evidence of a sustained
global mode.
Convective instability, local absolute instability, and global
instability describe different stages of disturbance develop-
ment. A disturbance introduced near the lower neutral radius
can first accumulate spatial growth as it travels downstream.
The frozen local flow may become absolutely unstable at a
larger radius. A sustained global response also depends on
radial development and the cavity boundaries. Receptivity
determines the initial modal amplitude by converting envi-
ronmental disturbances into instability modes 31. Acoustic or
vortical fluctuations, distributed roughness, localized surface
defects, and wall motion can produce different initial ampli-
tudes even when the modal growth rate is unchanged. The
downstream disturbance level depends on both the initial am-
plitude and the accumulated spatial growth. For the same
growth region, a small initial amplitude remains within the
linear regime longer. A larger initial amplitude reaches non-
linear development and secondary instability earlier. This dis-
tinction is especially important for the rotor layer. Its interval
between convective onset and the local absolute threshold is
much longer than that of the stator layer13,15,28.
Direct numerical simulation (DNS), asymptotic analysis,
and adjoint methods provide different ways to study recep-
tivity and disturbance development. DNS solves the Navier–
Stokes equations with a prescribed disturbance. It can follow
the response from linear growth to nonlinear saturation and
transition32. However, the cost of DNS limits broad parameter
studies. Classical asymptotic analysis and parabolized stabil-
ity equations reduce the cost for specific disturbance classes.
They can also retain slow radial development 9,31. Adjoint
methods provide a complementary linear approach. Once the
direct and adjoint systems have been solved for a selected
mode, many prescribed disturbances can be evaluated through
projection33–35. The full linearized Navier–Stokes problem
does not need to be solved again for each forcing distribution.
Adjoint receptivity analysis has recently been applied to
rotating disk flow. Thomas and Davies formulated the re-
sponse of stationary crossflow vortices to surface roughness35.
Thomas then extended the cylindrical adjoint formulation to
stationary and traveling Type I and Type II disturbances ex-
cited by momentum and mass sources or wall motion 36. For
an isolated disk, the Type I response is strongest when forcing
is concentrated near the critical layer, whereas the Type II re-
sponse is associated with the region where the effective shear
vanishes. Together, these studies relate the adjoint structure to
established characteristics of the two instability modes for an
isolated disk. Building on this framework, the present study
extends the receptivity analysis to the rotor layer in a rotor–
stator system. In this configuration, the rotating core modifies
the base flow and the instability modes. The downstream dis-
turbance level is then determined by combining local recep-
tivity with subsequent spatial growth.
Surface roughness provides a practical example of this dis-
tinction. Experiments on an isolated rotating disk connected
roughness with stationary crossflow vortices, and simulations
followed their growth, saturation, and breakdown 32,37. In a
rotor–stator cavity, Du et al. found that roughness first excites
stationary crossflow vortices and that traveling spiral waves
become dominant farther downstream 38. These observations
separate the disturbance pathway into initial excitation, lin-
ear spatial growth, and later modal interaction or nonlinear
development. The first two stages require both a local recep-
tivity coefficient and the spatial growth accumulated from the
forcing radius. For the confined rotor layer, combining these
quantities shows how surface mass flux changes the down-
stream disturbance level.
This extension is examined with a local linear analysis. The
base flow calculation first characterizes the changes produced
in the rotor layer by core rotation and surface mass flux. Tem-
poral and spatial calculations then determine the growth of
Type I and Type II, while a local absolute threshold provides a
downstream reference radius. Direct and adjoint modes quan-
tify the response to a localized wall displacement, and spa-
tial integration gives the subsequent disturbance amplitude.
The same prescribed wall displacement is also applied to the
von Kármán boundary layer to compare the confined and iso-
lated configurations. This sequence connects the change in
the base flow with modal growth, initial excitation, and down-
stream development.
The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Sec-
tion II presents the base flow model and its principal features.
Section III describes the convective and absolute instability
analyses, the adjoint problem, and the receptivity model. Sec-
tion IV presents the numerical results, and Sec. V summarizes
the conclusions.

B. Convective stability
Convective instability is characterized with complementary
temporal and spatial eigenvalue problems. In the temporal
formulation, α and β are real and ω j = ωr, j +iωi, j is complex;
ωi, j > 0 denotes temporal growth. In the spatial formulation,
ω and β are real and α j = αr, j + iαi, j is complex. The normal
mode factor exp [i(α jr + nθ − ωt)] gives a radial amplitude
proportional to exp(−αi, jr), so −αi, j > 0 denotes downstream
spatial growth. At neutral stability, ωi = 0 in the temporal

same real neutral state. The two formulations therefore share
one Zarf curve for each value ofas, while ωi and −αi quantify
the temporal growth rate and spatial growth rate away from

C. Absolute instability

that develop while propagating in the radial direction. Their
behavior at a fixed radius is determined by the spatiotem-

Local absolute instability begins when two spatial branches
originating in opposite halves of the complex α plane form a
Briggs pinch point with ωi > 0. Following this pinch point
as R increases identifies the radius at which the local response
changes from convective to absolute instability.

considered next, and the variation of its local receptivity with
surface mass flux, reduced frequency, and azimuthal mode
number is examined. Figure 19 maps the combined effects

The n = 40 curve has a lower peak near R ≈ 520, whereas
the n = 50 and 60 coefficients continue to increase over the
calculated interval. Increasing the reduced frequency lowers
these peaks and shifts the strongest radial variation outward.

tionary roughness therefore gives the largest local receptivity
response, with the largest coefficient occurring for n = 30 in

The three columns show how surface mass flux modifies
this reduced-frequency and mode-number dependence. Under
stationary forcing, injection increases |Cr|, raises the n = 40
peak to approximately 0.29, and shifts the n = 30 maximum
inward to R ≈ 390 [Fig. 19(a)]. Suction reduces the n = 30
peak to below 0.20, weakens the differences among n = 30,
40, and 50, and shifts the high-coefficient region of the higher
mode numbers outward [Fig. 19(c)]. The nonzero-frequency
rows retain the same overall contrast, although their coeffi-
cients are lower. Surface mass flux therefore changes both
the magnitude of local modal excitation and the radial range
over which each reduced-frequency and mode-number com-
bination produces a large receptivity coefficient.
The local receptivity trends do not by themselves determine
downstream disturbance levels. Figure 20 therefore compares
|Cr| with the spatial growth rate for three disturbances and
marks the lower neutral point of each case.
At the lower neutral points marked by the circles, −αi = 0
and |Cr| is below its largest value farther downstream. As R
increases from these points,−αi becomes positive and the dis-
turbances begin to grow. Over the same radial range, |Cr| can
also increase. Moving the forcing downstream can therefore
give a larger initial coefficient, but it leaves a shorter interval
for spatial growth.
The case ( ¯ω, n) = (0,30) shows this difference clearly. Its
lower neutral point is Rl = 287.822. The spatial growth rate
rises to its highest value atRg = 372.989. It then decreases but
remains positive. The coefficient |Cr| continues to increase
until Rp = 468.910. Forcing at Rp gives the largest initial co-
efficient, but it misses the spatial growth from Rl to Rp.
The two quantities have different roles. The coefficient|Cr|
gives the initial amplitude produced by wall forcing, whereas
−αi gives the local spatial growth rate. Figure 21 combines
them for (as, Reh, ¯ω, n) = ( 0,1000,0,30). The forcing ra-
dius R f is sampled from Rl to 500, primarily at intervals of
∆R f = 25; the exact characteristic positions Rg and Rp are
also included. Each disturbance is then followed to the local
Type I absolute instability thresholdRabs = 570. The N-factor
measures the accumulated spatial growth from R f , while the
overall amplitude A combines this growth with the initial co-
efficient Cr(R f ).
At the three characteristic forcing positions Rl, Rg, and Rp,
the endpoint N-factors are 4.336, 2.545, and 0.426, respec-
tively. The corresponding values of |A(570; R f )| are 10.973,
2.471, and 0.374. Thus, the disturbance introduced at the
neutral point gives the largest N-factor and amplitude, the
maximum-growth position gives the second largest values,
and the maximum-receptivity position gives the smallest val-
ues among the three. This ordering occurs even though |Cr|
increases from 0.1436 at Rl to its maximum of 0.2444 at Rp.
The additional black curves show that this result is not re-
stricted to the three characteristic positions. Across the sam-
pled forcing locations, both N(570; R f ) and |A(570; R f )| de-
crease monotonically as R f increases; at R f = 500, they have
fallen to 0.253 and 0.206, respectively. The longer spatial
growth interval available to upstream forcing therefore out-
weighs the downstream increase in local receptivity. Equiv-
alently, the lower neutral point produces the largest endpoint
amplitude among the sampled forcing locations. Neither the
local receptivity coefficient nor the local spatial growth rate
alone determines the downstream response; the initial excita-
tion and accumulated spatial growth must be considered to-
gether through A and the N-factor.
V. CONCLUSIONS
This study connects confinement, surface mass flux, modal
instability, and receptivity in the rotor boundary layer of a
rotor–stator cavity. The base flow is resolved across the full
gap, after which local temporal, spatial, and absolute instabil-
ity analyses identify the unstable modes. Adjoint projection
quantifies their excitation by localized wall displacement, and
spatial integration follows the resulting disturbances down-
stream. This sequence links the modification of the base flow
to modal growth, local forcing, and downstream disturbance
development.
The rotating core is the principal distinction from an iso-
lated disk. At Reh = 1000, the rotor and stator layers are
separated by a core with Gc = 0.313 and Π ≃ −0.098. As
Reh increases, the two wall layers become nearly indepen-
dent for Reh > 800, while the rotating core remains the outer
flow condition for the rotor layer. This condition modifies the
rotor velocity profiles and gradients in z. Surface mass flux
changes both the core and rotor layer: injection thickens the
layer, whereas suction thins it and increases total wall shear.
The associated changes in F ′, G′, and Sw modify the linear
stability operator. They therefore affect modal stability and
receptivity.
The two modes respond differently to these base flow
changes. The temporal neutral domains contain both the in-
viscid Type I and viscous Type II branches. Type II becomes
unstable at a smaller radius and occupies a broader radial do-\n16

For the representative conditions examined, the adjoint
fields have distinct structures across the layer. The Type I
adjoint mode is concentrated near the critical layer, whereas
the principal Type II peak occurs where the effective shear
vanishes. Under the same prescribed stationary wall displace-
ment, the isolated disk and rotor layers have similar peak lo-
cal receptivity coefficients, but the rotor maximum occurs at
a smaller radius. Surface mass flux also changes the radial
range and magnitude of the local receptivity response: injec-
tion increases the coefficients and shifts the largest response
inward, whereas suction reduces the peak response and shifts
the region of high coefficients for the higher mode numbers
outward.
The downstream results distinguish local excitation from
subsequent spatial growth. Under the same prescribed sta-
tionary wall displacement introduced at their respective lower
neutral radii, the disturbance over the isolated disk eventually
grows faster than that in the rotor layer despite the similar
peak local receptivity coefficients. Within the rotor layer, the
refined forcing location scan shows that both N(570; R f ) and
|A(570; R f )| decrease monotonically across the sampled forc-
ing radii from the lower neutral point toR f = 500. Among the
three characteristic positions, forcing at Rl, Rg, and Rp gives
endpoint amplitudes of 10.973, 2.471, and 0.374, respectively.
The neutral point forcing therefore gives the largest endpoint
amplitude over the sampled locations, even though it has the
smallest initial receptivity coefficient of the three. Its longer
spatial growth interval outweighs the larger local excitation
available farther downstream. The ordering at these three po-
sitions is consistent with the monotonic trend across all sam-
pled forcing radii and shows that the strongest local receptiv-
ity and the largest downstream disturbance need not occur at
the same radius.
The combined results show that disturbance development
depends on both modal growth and local receptivity. Sur-
face mass flux changes the base flow, shifts unstable domains,
alters the projection of wall forcing onto each mode, and
changes the radial interval available for spatial growth. Within
the present local linear framework, these effects describe how
injection, suction, and rotor–stator confinement affect the dis-
turbance response of the rotor boundary layer. The resulting
framework provides a consistent basis for distinguishing local
modal excitation from the downstream spatial growth of the
forced response.
