3 Numerical scheme, results and discussions
Several methods are at our disposal for solving the system of equations (10–15). For instance, for the incom-
pressible version of these equations, compact-ﬁnite differences were used by [15,17], a fourth-order Runge-
Kutta method was employed by [20,53] and a spectral tau technique was applied by [23,33]. We here prefer, due
primarily to its high accuracy, the use of spectral collocation method incorporating Chebyshev polynomials.
Having mapped the physical semi-inﬁnite region onto the computational domain via a suitable transformation,
a staggered mesh is then chosen so that Chebyshev Collocation points are interchanged between the Gauss–
Lobatto points (at which continuity equation (10) is imposed) and Gauss points (at which the momentum,
state and energy equations (11–15) are imposed), whenever necessary, particularly to deal with the pressure

perturbation at the wall, see, for instance [54]. As a result of the spectral discretization, the system (10–15)
together with the relevant boundary conditions were put into a large matrix form and then solved using LU
matrix decomposition combined with a usual Newton–Raphson search procedure. More detailed information
on the numerical technique adopted may be found in [24].
A ﬂow ﬁeld governed by the Navier–Stokes equations can sustain in general three types of modal wave
ﬁelds namely, acoustic, vorticity and entropy waves. In the linear approximation these modal ﬁelds are uncou-
pled except possibly at direct resonances. The instability of the boundary-layer ﬂow over a rigid rotating-disk
and a consequent transition route to turbulence are almost certain to occur through three distinct families of
eigensolutions created by the aforementioned physical waves and identiﬁed so far from the system of equations
(10–15). These are respectively the inviscid mode of [6], the viscous mode balanced by the Coriolis effects and
a third family computed ﬁrst in [13]. These modes were separately investigated by many researchers as out-
lined in the introduction. When these modes act together and thus constitute a coalescence in the wavenumber
plane, they lead to two well-known strong instability mechanisms. One is the local absolute instability which
takes place by the process of pinching of inviscid and viscous modes, a subject investigated by [20,21,23–25,
53]. These researches show that the incompressible rotating-disk boundary-layer ﬂow is exposed to a critical
Reynolds number of about 507 for the occurrence of absolute instability, though this speciﬁc Reynolds number
is pushed forward by the compressibility, see [32], and also the wall compliance has a powerful delaying inﬂu-
ence on the onset, see [23]. On the other hand, the modal coalescence taken place via the gathering of inviscid
mode and the mode of [13] causes a resonance at radial positions, plainly termed direct spatial resonance. The
work of [1] inherently reveals that such a resonance occurs at a critical Reynolds number of 445, much earlier
than the appearance of the absolute instability. Moreover, the direct numerical simulation ﬁndings of [3] as
well as the recent experimental study of [4,5] openly support that the global behavior of the instability in the
three-dimensional rotating-disk boundary-layer ﬂow is in compliance with the algebraic growth induced by
the direct resonance instability.
We below explore the occurrence of direct spatial resonance in the compressible viscous boundary-layer
ﬂow over a rotating-disk. First of all, we reproduced in Fig. 2a the resonance modes of [1] corresponding to
the incompressible ﬂow, which demonstrates an excellent agreement (see Fig. 3b of [1]). The effects of com-
pressibility, heat transfer as well as wall insulation are then investigated at some speciﬁed Prandtl numbers in
Figs. 2b, 3, 4, 5, 6 and 7a,b on which the variations with Reynolds numbers of the resonance parameters are dis-
played. Before presentation of the results, a few point deserves emphasizing. First, for the modes investigated
in the present study resonance occur for all the parameters, that is, not only the dispersion relation ensuing from
the system of equations (10–15) but also its rate of change with respect to complex radial wavenumber at the
determined real frequencies vanish. Second, it is, too, worthy of noting that the results displayed in Figs. 2b,
3, 4, 5, 6 and 7a,b are consistent since they converge to the incompressible limit asM
r tends to zero. Third, it
should also be reminded that the degeneracies exhibiting temporal growth of the amplitudes are not allowed
in this study, focussing only on the degeneracies leading to a spatial resonance and thus growth. Moreover,
the damping rate of a degeneracy should, as a rule, be low enough for it to exhibit growth. And lastly, the
Reynolds number of a degeneracy should correspond to the laminar region for it to contribute to the transition
mechanism.
